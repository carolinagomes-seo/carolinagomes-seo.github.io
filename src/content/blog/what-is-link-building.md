**Link building is the work of getting other websites to link to yours.** Search engines read those links as votes of trust: a link from a relevant, respected site tells Google your page is worth showing. It's the biggest part of off-page SEO, and today it also helps AI tools like ChatGPT and Google's AI Overviews recognize your brand.

> **About the author:** I'm Ana Carolina Gomes, and I've worked in link building since 2023, running outreach and media partnerships for websites in international, high-competition markets. I've built 250+ links per quarter. In this guide, I explain how link building works in plain words.

**Key takeaways**

- A link is a vote of trust. One relevant link beats dozens of random ones.
- Good links come from real sites, in real content, about a related topic.
- Buying links that pass ranking value breaks Google's rules.
- Link building is a process: find the right sites, offer them value, check every placement.
- Links and brand mentions also help AI tools connect your name to your topic.

## Why do links matter for SEO and AI search?

Links help search engines find your pages, signal that other people trust you, bring real visitors and help AI tools connect your brand to your topic. Content and technical SEO make a page good. Links are how the rest of the web says so.

- **Discovery.** Search engine crawlers move around the web by following links. A new page with no links pointing to it is hard to find. If you're new to crawling and indexing, start with [*What is SEO?*](/blog/what-is-seo/)
- **Trust.** A link from a respected site in your field works like a recommendation. That's why Google treats links meant only to manipulate rankings as spam: real links carry weight because they're earned.
- **Traffic.** A link in a popular article sends readers to your site, even when it doesn't pass any ranking value.
- **Brand signals for AI.** AI tools build answers from what the web says about a topic. When respected sites link to and mention your brand, it's easier for those tools to connect your name to your subject.

One thing I see often: people treat links as the whole of SEO. They aren't. A link pointing to a weak page rarely saves it, so the content has to be worth linking to first.

## What makes a good link?

A good link comes from a real, relevant site, sits inside useful content and makes sense to a reader. If you'd be happy for a potential customer to find the link and click it, it's probably a good one.

These are the checks I run on every site before I accept a placement:

| What to check | A good sign | A warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | The site and the article are about your topic | The site writes about everything, from crypto to recipes |
| Real audience | It gets organic traffic and has readers who comment or share | Traffic is close to zero, or dropped suddenly |
| Editorial context | The link sits in the body of an article, where it helps the reader | The link is in a footer, sidebar or a list of unrelated links |
| Anchor text | Natural words, like your brand name or a short description | An exact keyword repeated across many sites |
| Outbound links | The site links out sparingly, to sources that make sense | Almost every post links to a different business |
| Site quality | Original content, clear authors, a real "About" page | Thin or copied posts, no author, "write for us" on every page |

No single metric decides this. Third-party scores like Domain Rating or Domain Authority are useful filters, but I've seen high-score sites with no real readers. I always open the site and read a few posts before saying yes.

## What types of links are there?

Links differ in two ways: the label in their code (the rel attribute) and the way they were earned. The label tells Google how much to trust the link; the way it was earned decides whether it's worth having at all.

**By label:** A normal link has no rel attribute and can pass ranking value. Sites add a rel value when they want to describe the link to Google, as Google explains in its guide to [qualifying outbound links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links):

| Attribute | What it means | Where you'll see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No attribute | A normal link the site stands behind | Editorial links in articles |
| `rel="sponsored"` | The link is an ad or a paid placement | Sponsored posts, affiliate links, ads |
| `rel="ugc"` | The link was added by users | Comments, forum posts |
| `rel="nofollow"` | The site doesn't want to vouch for the link | Sites that link out but don't endorse |

Google says links with these attributes are generally not followed, though they can still bring readers.

**By source:** Editorial links happen when a writer links to you because your page helps their article. The rest come from outreach, and the tactics table below compares the most common ways to earn them.

## How does link building work, step by step?

Link building works as a repeatable process: choose what you want links to, find the right sites, offer them something useful, then check and measure every link. This is the process I follow every week.

1. **Pick the pages that deserve links.** Start with pages that are useful and matter to the business, like a guide, a tool, or a key service page.
2. **Research who could link to you.** Look at which sites link to your competitors, which publications cover your topic, and which pages already rank for your keywords. Backlink tools, even free versions, make this much faster.
3. **Vet every site.** Run the checks from the table above. In my experience, this step saves more money than any other, because a bad link costs the same as a good one.
4. **Reach out with something useful.** A short, personal email that explains why your content helps their readers works better than a template. Offer an article idea, a data point, or a fix for a broken link.
5. **Brief the content.** If you're writing a guest post, agree on the topic, the target page, and a natural anchor text before anyone writes a word.
6. **Check the placement.** When the link goes live, confirm the URL, the anchor, the rel attribute, and that the page can be indexed.
7. **Track and report.** Record every link with its date, site, target page, and cost, so you can see what worked.

**What this looks like at scale:** Two habits made the biggest difference. First, strict vetting: balancing link quality with acquisition cost brought the cost per link down by about 25%. Second, automation: writing a brief for each placement by hand was slow and easy to get wrong, so I built a workflow with Claude Code and Python that creates the briefs automatically.

## Which link building tactics work today?

The tactics that work today all give the linking site a reason to link: better information, a fix, or a story. Here's how they compare, from my experience:

| Tactic | How it works | Effort | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linkable assets | Create something people want to cite: original data, a free tool, a detailed guide | High at first, then links keep coming | Sites ready to invest in one strong piece |
| Digital PR | Pitch a newsworthy story or data to journalists | High | Brands with data, expertise, or a timely angle |
| Guest posts | Write a useful article for a relevant site | Medium | Building authority in a specific niche |
| Broken link building | Find dead links on relevant pages and offer your page instead | Medium | Sites with strong guides or resources |
| Unlinked mentions | Ask sites that already name your brand to add a link | Low | Brands people already talk about |
| Resource pages | Ask to be included in curated lists on your topic | Low to medium | Tools, templates, and in-depth guides |

If you're just starting, begin with unlinked mentions and resource pages. They're the quickest wins. Then put your energy into one linkable asset, because good assets keep earning links long after you publish them.

## What should you avoid?

Avoid any link created mainly to manipulate rankings. Google calls this link spam, and its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) say sites that break them may rank lower or disappear from results. The policy now also covers attempts to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search.

Google's examples of link spam include:

- Buying or selling links that pass ranking value, including paying with money, products, or services
- Excessive link exchanges ("link to me and I'll link to you")
- Using automated programs or services to create links
- Paid articles, guest posts, or press releases with links that pass ranking value or use keyword-stuffed anchors
- Low-quality directory and bookmark sites
- Links hidden in widgets, footers, or templates spread across many sites
- Forum comments with optimized links

There's an important nuance. Google says buying and selling links is a normal part of advertising, and it's allowed when the link carries `rel="sponsored"` or `rel="nofollow"`. The problem is paying for links that pass ranking value without telling anyone.

My rule of thumb: if a deal only makes sense because of what it does to rankings, and you'd be uncomfortable explaining it to the site's readers, don't do it.

## How is link building connected to digital PR and AI search?

Digital PR is one way to do link building, and both now feed the same goal: making your brand a trusted name in your topic, for search engines and for AI tools.

|  | Link building | Digital PR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main goal | Links that improve rankings | Media coverage and brand awareness |
| Typical targets | Blogs, niche sites, resource pages | News sites, journalists, industry media |
| What you offer | Useful content, a fix, a guest article | A story, data, or an expert comment |
| Main result | Backlinks to specific pages | Links plus brand mentions and reach |

In practice, I use both. Outreach and partnerships bring steady links to the pages that need them; digital PR brings links from sites that are hard to reach any other way.

The AI side is where this gets interesting. Tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overviews tend to cite sources that other trusted sites already reference. So a brand that is linked and mentioned across respected sites in its field has a better chance of showing up in AI answers. That's why I think of links and mentions as the off-page half of GEO (generative engine optimization).

## How do you measure link building results?

Measure link building in two layers: the links themselves, and what they changed for the pages they point to. Counting links alone tells you how busy you were, not whether it worked.

| Metric | What it tells you | Where to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New referring domains | How many different sites link to you | Google Search Console (Links report), Ahrefs Webmaster Tools |
| Link quality | Whether links come from relevant, real sites | Your own vetting notes |
| Cost per link | How efficient your process is | Your link tracking sheet |
| Rankings and impressions of target pages | Whether the links helped the pages you chose | Google Search Console |
| Referral traffic | Whether people actually click the links | Google Analytics |
| Brand mentions and AI citations | Whether your brand is becoming part of the conversation | Manual checks in AI tools, alerts for your brand name |

You can track most of these with free tools; I explain how in [my guide to free SEO tools](/blog/free-seo-tools/).

Give it time. Links usually take weeks or months to show an effect on rankings, so compare results over a quarter, not a week. That's also why I review cost per link and quality every quarter, not after each placement.

## FAQ

### Is link building still important?

Yes. Links still help search engines judge which pages to trust, but quality now matters far more than quantity.

### How many backlinks do I need?

There's no fixed number. Look at how many referring domains the pages ranking for your keyword have, and focus on getting relevant links rather than matching a count.

### Are nofollow links worth anything?

Yes. They usually don't pass ranking value, but a nofollow link on a popular site still brings readers and brand visibility. A natural link profile has a mix of both.

### Can I do link building without a budget?

Yes. Unlinked mentions, resource pages and one piece of content worth citing cost more time than money.

## Final thoughts: where should you start?

Link building rewards patience and honesty more than tricks. Start with one page worth linking to, go after the easy wins first, and track every link from day one. Done consistently, this helps both your rankings and your visibility in AI answers.
